A. Warm-Up

You own every verb the robot needs. Today you decide where they point, on paper, before the wheels move.

What you already own

Verb What it does
🧭 robot.reset(angle=0) “the way I’m facing now is zero”
📏 drive_straight(mm) drive straight, or backward if the number is negative
🎯 robot.turn(h, absolute=True) turn until you face heading h

🤔 Three verbs, and a mat with twelve jobs on it. The verbs are solved. The order and the numbers are not.

⏱️ Time: 10 minutes

Open by making the inventory feel finished — the driving problem is closed, and that is exactly why today is a planning day, not a coding day. Nothing new gets typed for the first hour. If a squad is itching to code, point at the gate: today’s deliverable is a written path with marked reset points, and squads that plan first will out-run squads that type first. Keep absolute=True visible on the board all session; the whole day hangs off the word face.

Today’s job

  • Turn the mat into numbers: a measured path, not a guessed one
  • Learn where the robot’s error gets erased, and put those points on purpose
  • Read a heading off a map and paste it into robot.turn(…, absolute=True) without it lying to you

Every run you have ever done started with someone guessing a distance and tuning it by eye. Today you stop guessing. 📐

B. Break It

Plan a route the way it feels natural, and watch it fall apart before the robot leaves the bench.

Write me a route 📝

Squad task, 4 minutes: on the mat printout, write the instructions to get from the start area to the microphone and back. Any words you like.

Almost every squad writes something like this:

go forward a bit
turn right
go forward until you're near the mic
turn left a little
grab it

Do NOT correct anything yet — collect three or four squads’ routes on the board verbatim, including the vague ones. The vagueness is the lesson and it has to be theirs. Expect “a bit”, “until you’re near”, “a little”, and — the one you are hunting for — “turn right”. Circle every relative turn word you see; leave the circles up, you will come back to them in two slides. Resist the urge to hand out the fix here.

Quick quiz 🚨

Your route says turn right 90, four times, around a rectangle. Each turn lands 3° hot. Where is the robot pointing at the end?

12° off, and it stays 12° off. Nothing in the plan ever measures the mistake, so nothing can give it back. The fourth side is driving into the weeds.

You met this exact bug on the square day. 🧠 The cure was the same then: stop saying turn 90 more, start saying face 90.

This is a callback, not new material — the blind square from Drive a Square failed for precisely this reason, and the “targets that climb” payoff at the end of that day is the fix. Make them say the number: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12, and the plan has no place to notice. Then land the sentence that runs the rest of the day: a relative turn banks its mistake; an absolute turn hands it back. If a squad protests “but I’d just tune the 90 to 87” — excellent, take it seriously, then ask what happens when the batteries drop and the scrub changes. Tuning a relative turn fixes one mat on one day.

The word you can’t write down ✍️

“Turn right” is not an instruction. It’s an instruction plus a secret: where you were already pointing.

turn_right(90)        # 90 from... where? the plan doesn't say
robot.turn(90, absolute=True)   # face 90. no secret.

A plan full of relative turns can only be read in order, from the start. Miss one step, and every number after it is wrong. 😬

Push on the “in order, from the start” line — it is the practical cost and they will feel it within the hour. With relative turns you cannot start a run in the middle to test one segment, you cannot reorder two jobs to save time, and you cannot debug step 7 without running steps 1 through 6. All three of those are things they will want to do today. Absolute headings make every step independently testable, which is the real reason the competition code uses them; error-absorption is the bonus.

C. A Map With a Zero

If “face 90” is the plan, something has to decide which way 90 is. That’s a map’s job.

Meet the field tool 🗺️

tools/odommap/OdomMap.html opens in a browser, works offline, and has the real 2026 RoboMission Elementary mat in it.

Mode What it gives you
📏 Normal click two points → distance in mm, wheel rotations
🧭 Odometry set an origin and a zero direction → every point as (x, y)
🛣️ Route click a multi-leg detour → total length

The mat is 2362 × 1143 mm. Set Wheel = 88 mm, the same number that lives in your ROBOT FACTS.

Have the tool already open on the projector and one laptop per squad — this is a hands-on block, not a demo. The wheel field matters: it converts every measured distance into wheel rotations, and if a squad’s robot has a different wheel they must type their number, not 88. Point out that 88 also lives in code/movements.py under ROBOT FACTS — same fact, two places, and the mat tool is the one that is allowed to be approximate because the mat is flat and the robot is not.

The bar IS the compass 🧭

In Odometry mode you click twice:

  1. the origin: “this corner is (0, 0)”
  2. the bar: you swing an arrow and click. “This direction is zero.”
heading: 0°
​
swing the bar, then press an absolute turn button, then watch where it lands

Run this from their predictions, every time: swing the bar somewhere ugly like 47°, ask “if I call robot.turn(90, absolute=True), how far does it turn?” — collect numbers, then click. Do it again from −120°. The yellow arc is the amount turned and it changes every time; the bar always finishes on the label. Say the sentence out loud and make them say it back: the turn changed, the destination did not. That is the whole of absolute=True in one line. The snap hint (within 10°) mirrors the real tool — OdomMap grabs 0/90/180/−90 so a hand-swung bar still lands on a clean axis; tell them to use that, because a mat laid square to the table has no business having a 3° origin.

Zero is a decision, not a fact

robot.reset(angle=0)  # "the way I'm facing NOW is zero"

Placing the bar in the tool and calling robot.reset(angle=0) on the mat are the same act, done in two places:

On the map On the mat
click the origin put the robot in the start area
swing the bar, click point it, robot.reset(angle=0)
every point is now (x, y) every absolute turn is now a promise

Point the bar the way the robot faces at the start, and the map’s numbers are your code’s numbers. Point it anywhere else and you’ll be adding an offset in your head all day. 😵

This is the slide that earns the day. Make the pairing physical: one student places the real robot in the start area while another places the origin and bar in the tool, and the room checks they agree. The failure mode to name out loud — a squad sets the bar along the mat’s long edge because it looks tidy, but starts the robot facing across the short edge, and now every heading in their plan is 90° out. It will look like a code bug for an hour. Nobody will find it in the code, because it is not in the code.

D. Two Maps, Two Zeros

There is a second set of headings on this mat, and it does not agree with yours.

The trap 🪤

The coach’s optimizer (tools/route_optimizer/field_data.py) also stores headings. It uses a different zero.

🌐 map & robot
0 = up · clockwise
≠
📊 field_data.py
0 = right · toward +Y

Both spin the same way round. They just start counting in different places, exactly 90° apart.

Do not skip this because it looks like plumbing — it is the single most expensive silent bug available on this mat, and it costs 15 anchor points with no error message. The reason the two differ is honest, not sloppy: field_data.py is desktop code doing geometry in image coordinates (origin top-left, x right, y down), where “angle from +X” is the natural convention; the robot is a robot, and a robot’s zero is the way it is facing. Neither is wrong. They are simply two frames, and the only sin is forgetting which one a number came from.

One line of arithmetic 🧮

map_heading = field_heading + 90

Check it on the two you can see:

field_data.py direction map & robot
0° → right 90°
90° ↓ down 180°

🏷️ Label every heading you write down. A number with no frame attached is not a measurement, it’s a rumour.

Derive it with them on the board rather than asserting it; two rows is enough and the table gives both. Then make the rule of thumb stick — in their notebooks, every heading gets a tag: 190 (map) or 100 (field). Coaches: this is also the review question to ask any squad that reports “the cable keeps landing sideways” — the first thing to check is not the mechanism, it is which frame the number came from. The conversion is a + 90 in one direction and a − 90 in the other; if a squad writes the minus version, that is correct too, and worth praising.

E. The Cable That Must Lie Down

A real 30-point job that a relative turn simply cannot describe.

Measure it yourself 📐

The grey area The cable
79.7 × 207.2 mm 128 mm long (2× Technic Brick 1×16)

Can the cable lie across the short side of the area?

No. 128 − 79.7 = 48.3 mm too long. It has to go the long way. The orientation isn’t a choice you get to make, because the geometry already made it. 🔒

Give them the two numbers and let them find the 48.3 themselves — it takes fifteen seconds and it converts an abstract rule into something they proved. This is the same “measure, don’t guess” move as the wheel diameter on D2. The consequence to draw out next is the one that surprises people: because the two grey areas are mirrored, a mechanism that lays both cables with the same motion gets exactly one of them right, every single time, and the scoresheet just quietly says 15 instead of 30.

Two areas, mirrored 🪞

CABLE_HEADING_DEG = {          # tools/route_optimizer/field_data.py
    "cable_upper": 100.0,      # field frame!
    "cable_lower":  80.0,      # field frame!
}

Both sit ±10° from vertical, tilted 20° apart from each other. Convert before you use them:

field + 90 map & robot
upper 100.0° → 190°
lower 80.0° → 170°

Yaw tolerance is ±31°, which is generous. A rough approach still scores if it aims at the right heading. Paste 100 straight into robot.turn(…, absolute=True) and you’re 90° out: sideways, and 128 mm won’t fit in 79.7. 💸

Land the arithmetic slowly — 190 and 170 are both ±10 from 180, which is straight down the mat, and that is the sanity check they should run on every converted number: does the answer point somewhere the mat actually agrees with? The ±31° tolerance is the good news and the trap in one figure: it is wide enough to forgive a sloppy drive, which is why a 90° frame error is so nasty — it is far outside tolerance, so it never half-works and never teaches you gradually. It just fails, cleanly, at 15 points. Tie it back to the anchor: cables are 30 of the ~135, and half of 30 is the difference between a gate that passes and one that doesn’t.

So the step has to name a heading

CABLE_UPPER_HEADING = 190     # facts about the MAT
CABLE_LOWER_HEADING = 170

robot.turn(CABLE_UPPER_HEADING, absolute=True)   # …then push
  • Two cables, two numbers, one step shape: not one number, and not two copies
  • The heading is a fact about the mat, so it lives with the other mat facts

Try writing this with a relative turn. You can’t, because “turn 20” only means something if you already know where you were. The mat doesn’t care where you were. 🤷

This closes the loop opened in section B: the circled “turn right” phrases on the board are now provably unwritable for this job. The design point for the stronger squads is that the two cables differ by a number, not by a behaviour — same lesson as drive_straight(distance_mm) — so the number gets a name and the step gets written once in shape. Do not let anyone paste 190 into one place and 170 into a near-identical copy of the same block; a bare 190 in the middle of a mission is a magic number that nobody will find when the mat is re-measured, and a second copy is the habit the constants block was built to kill. If a squad proposes wrapping the turn-and-push into push_cable(heading), say the honest thing: that is a good instinct and it is deferred, not wrong. Three lines that appear twice do not yet earn a name in a 5-week course, and hiding the mission behind mission-named verbs is what stops a student reading their own run; composing verbs is a continuation-season job (decision #60). What they must take away today is the heading, which is worth 15 points and cannot be recovered later.

F. Lab & Gate

Lab: draw the anchor run 🖍️

On your mat printout, mark every one of these:

  1. 🏁 the origin and the zero direction (the same one your code will reset to)
  2. 📍 each job in order: instruments 45 · cables 30 · avoidance 40 · mic 20
  3. 🧭 the heading for every leg, tagged (map)
  4. 🩹 every reset point: a wall square or a line, where error goes back to zero

Rule of thumb from the optimizer: don’t dead-reckon more than about 3 m without erasing error somewhere. 🧽

Circulate with the printouts and hunt for three specific things. One: an unlabelled heading — ask “map or field?” and enjoy the pause. Two: a long stretch with no reset point, which is where their run will actually die on competition day. Three: a fragile step placed late, after points are already banked — the ordering principle is never put a shaky step where its failure costs points you already earned. The ~3 m figure comes from the optimizer’s dead-reckoning cap; it is a planning heuristic, not a hard rule, and squads with a well-tuned gyro will beat it. Time-box this hard at 25 minutes or it eats the session.

Competition: Cartographers 🗺️

The judge names a job on the mat. 90 seconds to hand back: the heading to face it (tagged), the distance in mm, and the nearest reset point you’d use.

Score: 1 point per correct number · +2 if the frame tag is right · −2 for an untagged heading 😈

Run three rounds so every squad gets a different job and nobody wins on one lucky memorised number. The −2 is deliberately harsh and you should announce it cheerfully — an untagged heading is worse than no heading, because the untagged one gets used. Judges: accept any heading within the ±31° yaw tolerance for cable jobs, and within ±10° elsewhere; you are scoring the plan, not their gyro. If a squad hands back a field-frame number correctly tagged (field) plus the conversion, that is full marks — the tag is the skill.
