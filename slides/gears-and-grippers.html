A. Break It

Yesterday you built a gripper. Today you understand it and make it score. 🦾

Frame the day in one sentence: “You can build it and you can run it — today you’ll be able to explain it and improve it.” That’s the ladder: follow → modify → create. The deck goes straight to the gripper — the confidence today comes from their own build, not from drills.

Feel it: the heavy lift 🏋️

Clamp something heavy, like a full water bottle. Lift.

What happens? Why? The motor is trying its hardest, so where is the strength going?

The motor isn’t weak. The ratio is. Your gears hand the claw exactly the strength the motor makes, 1 : 1.

The engineered failure that makes gear theory land as a rescue instead of a lecture. With the 1:1 bevel pairs, a genuinely heavy object slips or stalls the medium motor — let them feel it strain, and don’t let a stall cook the motor for more than a moment. Then the hook question: “a car engine can’t lift a car either — yet the jack in the trunk lifts it with one hand. What’s the trick?” Someone will say gears or leverage — that someone just introduced Act B for you.

Break it: two claws, one brain 🧠

Carry the bottle in the top claw. Now open the bottom claw without moving the top one.

Try. What happens? Is this a feature or a bug?

Both. One motor → two claws is a motor saved, and 4 motors is the Elementary cap (§5.2.8). The price is that they always move together. Engineering is knowing the price. 🏷️

The coupling discovery, on their own design decision. Name the budget correctly, because this is where it is usually got wrong: our robot has a spare port (A); what it does not have is a spare motor — F and B drive, E and C grip, and Elementary allows 4, maximum (§5.2.8). A second gripper motor is what we cannot afford, not a second plug.

Praise the trade before naming its cost, then plant the question that decides whether the price is affordable: does any moment in the anchor run need “top claw holding” AND “bottom claw moving” at once? Have squads check against the cables → mic sequence. If yes, the fixes are geometry (each claw engages in a different part of the rotation range) or making one claw passive — a design conversation for the build block. Point at the build itself: have a squad trace motor shaft → axle → gear pair → claw arm with a finger, and the coupling is right there in their hands.

B. Name It: The Gear Dictionary

Everything you just felt has a name and a number. 🔢

Count teeth, know the future

Every gear question starts the same way: count the teeth.

Ratio = follower teeth ÷ driver teeth.

Bevel gears buy direction, not strength. Your gripper spent its gears on the corner, so the lift failed.

Teeth = size: meshing gears share tooth size. It’s a ratio, so unlike the wheel, π cancels. Just count. 🔢

Keep the arithmetic tiny: teeth in, teeth out, one division. Have every squad physically count the teeth on a spare bevel gear — counting beats being told. The reveal sentence is the punchline of Act A: their gear pair spends its entire budget on turning the axis 90° (which the build needs — motor lies flat, claws swing on vertical axles) and has nothing left for strength. Now the fix is obvious before it’s taught: change the ratio.

The trade: speed ⇄ strength

driver 24T → follower:

Small drives big → slower but stronger. Speed and strength are one budget, and gears decide how to spend it.

Click through the three followers and narrate the yellow spokes — the driver is always the 24T, only the follower changes. 12T: the claw spins twice as fast and grips half as hard. 24T: exactly the motor, no bonus — the same 1:1 deal their own bevel pair gives them. 36T: gives up a third of its speed and buys half again the strength (×1.5). One sentence carries the slide: “speed and strength are the same budget — gears just move money between the accounts.”

Then link each direction to a mission verb: gear for torque where a push needs force (cable pusher against friction), for speed where it needs reach (a claw that must open wide in a hurry). Physical demo beats the animation if time allows: hold a 12T driving a 36T between two fingers and feel the follower resist. Resist the full gear zoo — idlers, racks, worms and compound trains are continuation-season material. Coach prep: WRO-Learn Fundamentals of Mechanics, PrimeLessons Robot Designs, Competition Ready L7 Time for an Upgrade.

Challenge: tune YOUR gripper 🛠️

Pick one, 20 minutes

  • 💪 Stronger: re-gear so the bottle lift succeeds, and prove it with the lift
  • ⚡ Faster: re-gear so open→close beats the stopwatch, and prove it with a timed cycle
  • Rules: same motor, same port, only the gears change
  • Still fits the 250 mm cube with claws closed 📏
  • Before you build: say the ratio out loud. Predict, then prove

Modification is their proven skill — this stretches it one step, with a prediction gate (“say the ratio before you touch a gear”) so the theory does the work. Squads choose their own goal; both choices are correct, which quietly teaches that gearing is a decision, not an answer. Watch for the classic trap: gearing so slow the claw takes 5 seconds to close — a run costs time. If a squad finishes early: “now make the two claws close at different speeds from the same motor” — a compound ratio discovered, not assigned. This is also where mechanism talent becomes visible post-draft; watch who leads the rebuild.

C. Make It Score

A gripper that lifts bottles is a toy. A gripper that scores 50 points is a mechanism. 💰

The jobs: cables + microphone 🎸🎤

Task Points Scores when…
🔌 Cables (×2) 30 (15 each) completely in the grey area and upright
🎤 Microphone 20 completely in the target area and upright

50 of the ~135 anchor, with zero color needed. You’ve already shoved cables with the bare chassis. Now the claw makes it repeatable.

Both lines come from the game rules via the plan’s scoring table — the operative word in both is upright, and upright is decided by mechanism geometry + approach angle, not by code. The mission previews planted this: squads already discovered that approach angle decides whether a shoved cable stands or falls. Map the two jobs onto their own two claws: the bottom sweep claw is a natural cable pusher, the top carry claw a natural mic carrier — the design was pointed at these tasks all along; today they find that out.

Pusher physics: three tricks that beat luck

Design rule: let the mechanism forgive what the driving can’t guarantee. ±5 mm of aim error should still score.

This slide is the difference between a build that works once and a mechanism that gates at ≥8/10. The funnel is tolerance engineering a 9-year-old can hold: the robot will never arrive perfectly, so build a mouth that makes imperfect arrivals perfect enough. Over-travel is its twin in code. Pros design the mechanism around the worst expected approach, not the best. The ±5 mm number is a design target to test against, not a rule — nudge their aim off on purpose and see if the funnel saves it. The third trick is the gear challenge coming home: torque where the push needs force, speed where the claw needs reach.

Break it: the claw that got shoved 💥

Close the claw. Now shove it back open with your hand, with the program still running.

Two squads, same nudge, one word different:

claw.run_angle(300, 150)     # squad A
claw.run_target(300, 150)    # squad B

Run it again. Do the two claws land in the same place? 👀

No. Squad A is 150° past the shove; squad B went back to the same spot it always uses. Same nudge, and one verb forgave you. 🤔

run_angle(300, 150) run_target(300, 150)
you say “turn 150° more” “go to 150”
counts from wherever it is right now zero
run it twice moves again, 300 total second one does nothing
after a shove inherits the shove ❌ drives it back out ✅

robot.turn(90, absolute=True) is a compass. run_angle is a steering wheel. 🧭 Wheels roll forever, so by is fine. A claw has a place it must be, so mechanisms use run_target. Its one trap is on your cheat sheet.

Ten minutes, and it must be felt, not told — the slide dies if you announce the answer. Get real predictions on the board first; most of the room will guess both claws end up wrong, because “I moved it with my hand” feels like it must break something. Then the reveal question: counts from where, exactly? run_angle counts from here — wherever the claw happens to be the instant the line runs, nudge included. run_target counts from a zero, and does not care how the claw got where it is. Hold the room in the confusion before you advance the fragment.

Two staging notes. Keep the program running (a loop with a button wait, or the REPL) — that is what makes squad B recover. And make the shove big and obvious, 30–40°; a 5° nudge looks like noise. If a squad’s claw is heavily geared down, their hand-shove may back-drive the gears rather than the motor — have them nudge the motor’s own axle instead.

Then build the table live, one row at a time, from what they just watched — it lands far harder filled in with the room than read off the screen. Row 3 is the cheapest to prove and the most convincing: run either line twice and run_angle walks the claw across the room while run_target sits there refusing to move because it has already arrived.

The absolute-turn callback is the sentence of the slide, and it is a genuine callback: the square day taught exactly this on the gyro, and the checkpoint’s hardest question turned on it. Say the general rule out loud, because it is the transferable one: a relative move inherits every mistake before it; an absolute move forgets them. That is also why movements.py deliberately has no “turn 90 from here” verb.

Say the sign trap out loud even though it is no longer on the slide — it costs an afternoon, it is on their cheat sheet, and this is the moment it makes sense: run_target ignores the sign of the speed, because the target picks the direction. Every other verb they own — run_angle, run, run_until_stalled — taught them “negative means the other way”, and this one quietly doesn’t. Show it: run_target(-300, 0) from an open claw closes it, exactly like the positive version, because 0 is on the same side either way. Point at the cheat sheet as you say it, so they learn where the answer lives.

So whose zero is it? 💥

run_target goes to 150. 150 measured from where?

Not from your jaws. From a mark on the motor shaft, picked by the hub the moment your program started.

Slip one gear tooth and the shaft mark stays put. Your jaws don’t.

You re-geared this claw an hour ago. Every angle in your notebook is now measured from a mark that moved. 😱

The code was never wrong. The zero was never yours. 🎯

This is the beat that earns home(), so don’t rush it — and it is a different failure from the shove two slides back, which is the point. A shove gets counted; the motor was awake and watching. What is not counted is anything that changes the relationship between the motor shaft and your jaws: a gear tooth skipping under load, an axle pulled and re-seated one notch round, and above all a re-gear, which is a thing this room did to itself this morning. Stage the cheapest version: pull the claw axle, put it back one tooth over, re-run the exact same line, watch the jaws land somewhere new with no edit to the code. Thirty seconds, impossible to argue with.

Say the ownership line slowly, because it is the whole argument: zero is a mark on the motor, and the motor has never seen your claw. That mismatch is fine right up until something between the two moves, and then it is silent, because a wrong zero produces perfectly confident wrong behaviour. Passes on the bench, dies on the mat, no error message.

Then hold the room in it. Do not give the fix. Someone will say “can’t it just find zero by itself?” — that sentence is the next slide, in their words, and it is worth waiting twenty seconds of silence for.

home(): go and touch your zero 🧱

def home():
    """Shut on nothing. THAT is zero."""
    claw.run_until_stalled(-CLOSE_SPEED, then=Stop.COAST, duty_limit=___)
    wait(200)                    # let the gears unwind before we believe it
    claw.reset_angle(___)        # jaws met = 0 -- every run, every robot
    claw.run_target(OPEN_SPEED, READY_ANGLE, then=Stop.COAST)
Part What it means
speed which way and how fast, where negative = the other way
then=Stop.COAST relax when you arrive
duty_limit how hard it may push, 0–100. Start at 40

Why shut and not open? Because “did I catch it?” is a question about the jaws meeting. Put your zero where the question is, and the answer is just “did we stop before 0?” 🎯

Blanks: duty_limit=40 and reset_angle(0). They already own run_until_stalled from the wall day, so the table is a reminder, not a lesson — spend the time on the datum instead.

The teal fragment is the whole slide. The claw could home either way; homing shut is the choice that deletes a number. Zero at fully-open means “did I catch something?” can only be answered against a measured landmark — how far away the jaws meet — which is a number you must measure, re-measure after every re-gear, and which is silently wrong in between. Zero at jaws met makes that number 0 by construction, so it cannot be mis-measured or go stale. Say the portable version: put the zero where the question is. This is the same move as find_line() for the wheels — stop guessing, go and touch something that is where it says it is — and the contrast is worth naming: the claw has a hard stop it can press against, so home() is exact; the mat only offers landmarks to look at, so find_line() fixes distance and nothing fixes heading.

Line 4 is the one they’ll want to delete — make them keep it. The gear train is still under load the instant the stall is detected, and reading the angle before it unwinds bakes a few degrees of backlash into zero, every run, in the same direction. Systematic error, not noise, and systematic errors survive into competition. Line 6 is why the claw ends up open and ready rather than clamped shut — READY_ANGLE is a number they choose (wide enough for the biggest thing they carry), not one they measure.

Say the scope line deliberately — duty_limit lives in home() and nowhere else, because it is a percentage of whatever power is available today, which is the same objection they already accepted against dc(). Finding a rigid stop can afford that sloppiness; deciding how hard to squeeze an object cannot. That distinction is the hinge the rest of the session turns on.

Assign it now as a hard habit: claw.home() is line one of every mission program from today onward, no exceptions, enforced at the gate. It costs one second. The four ways homing goes wrong are staged in the build block, not here — don’t pre-empt them.

Break it: the claw that lets go 💥

Grip it by stalling. Then take the robot for a ride.

claw.run_until_stalled(-300, then=Stop.HOLD, duty_limit=50)
drive_straight(600)
robot.turn(90, absolute=True)

The claw never opened. So how did the bottle get out? 🍾

Stalling stopped where it stopped, then held that spot. The bottle settled 1 mm, and the squeeze went to zero. 😱

Ten minutes, and it has to be felt — this is the break-it the whole grip arc hangs on, and the failure they will otherwise meet for the first time in front of a judge. Everyone grips by stalling, everyone drives, and most claws will shed the object on the turn; if a squad’s doesn’t, have them tap the object once mid-carry, or use something rounder.

Sit in the confusion before explaining: the claw is visibly still closed, the code visibly never opened it, so the object cannot be gone — and yet it is. The reveal is the difference between HOLDING A POSITION and HOLDING A FORCE, worth drawing on the board: Stop.HOLD after a stall means “stay at this angle”, so the instant the object shifts a hair, the error is zero, the motor relaxes, and the claw is merely resting on the object. Ask what the motor would have to do instead to keep pressing — someone will say “keep closing”, and that is the next slide, in their words.

Do not let the session frame stalling as bad — it is still exactly right for home(), and the distinction (rigid frame vs. a squashy object that moves) is the transferable idea.

Grip is a force, not a place 🤝

claw.control.limits(torque=___)      # the squeeze -- set ONCE

def grab():
    claw.run_target(CLOSE_SPEED, GRIP_TARGET, then=Stop.HOLD, wait=False)
    while not claw.stalled() and not claw.done():
        wait(10)            # no hold(), no stop() -- that IS the squeeze

GRIP_TARGET sits a few degrees past where the jaws meet. The claw can never get there, and that’s the point. It never stops trying. 🎯

Never call hold() here. A motor that arrives stops pushing. 🚫

Blank is torque=GRIP_TORQUE, and the units matter: mNm, an amount of real turning force, not a percentage. Make them say why that beats duty_limit — they already accepted the argument about dc(), and this is the same argument in a different hat: a percentage is a promise about the battery, a unit is a promise about the robot.

Teach the aim-past trick with hands, not words: hold a squad’s claw half-closed and ask one student to keep squeezing while you slowly let the object shrink. To keep pressure they have to keep moving, which is exactly what an unreachable target buys. And note that the unreachable target is now trivially easy to state — zero is where the jaws meet, so “past the jaws” is just a small negative number, the same in every squad’s file.

The wait loop is the one piece of plumbing worth a sentence, not a slide: wait=False starts the squeeze and hands the program straight back, and the loop then waits for the jaws to stop moving — stalled on the object, or shut on air. Open the file if anyone asks; don’t spend the room’s attention on the loop.

The purple fragment is the trap they will otherwise fall into: several will “tidy up” by adding claw.hold() at the end, which silently restores the exact bug the previous slide staged. Say it out loud: the move is left running on purpose. Contrast with lift_gripper.py if anyone built a fork — a fork asks for a height and means it, because gravity does the holding, so position control is right there and wrong here. Same verbs, opposite reasoning.

Two new jobs, and no new verbs 📦

# the cable -- push PAST the line, then leave it standing
robot.turn(CABLE_UPPER_HEADING, absolute=True)
drive_straight(TO_CABLE_MM + ___)     # over-travel: how far past?
drive_straight(-BACKOFF_MM)

# the mic -- arrive, let go GENTLY, retreat
drive_straight(TO_MIC_MM)
claw.release(___)                     # how far open? just enough to let go
drive_straight(-BACKOFF_MM)

Every line is a verb you already own. Six lines, zero defs: these are list entries, not library. 🗂️

Tempted to call it push_cable()? Ask the wrapper test: does the name buy a new behaviour, or just hide three lines you can already read? 🧐

The blanks are the point — the structure is given (their level), the numbers are theirs to find (the stretch): over-travel ≈ 20–30 mm, found on the mat, not chosen at the desk; and the number inside release() is what decides whether the mic lands upright, because a claw that swings all the way out to READY_ANGLE sweeps its jaw arms straight through it, and “upright” is where the 20 points live — smallest angle that still lets go, found on the mat.

The slide’s real move is the one it refuses to make. Last season these six lines were handed over as push_cable() and place_mic(), and that was wrong for the same reason a still_holding() wrapper was wrong the season before it — it existed for one term and did nothing but call the verb underneath, which taught the room that two names meant two behaviours and sent students hunting for a difference that was never there (#40). A name that wraps three calls you can already read buys you nothing and costs you the ability to see the run. Expect someone to propose the wrapper — they should, it’s the habit we drilled — and answer with the wrapper test on the slide. drive_straight earns its name (it hides the DriveBase settings dance); push_cable doesn’t (it hides a turn and two drives). Say plainly that this is a deferral, not a ban: when they have retyped the same three lines in four missions, that is the day the name is earned — in the continuation season, with the mission dictionary.

Filing rule: driving verbs live in movements.py; home, grab and release live in a gripper file — claw_gripper.py and/or lift_gripper.py, one per mechanism the robot actually carries; the cable and the mic live in the mission program, as steps, because they are the mission and nothing else.

Build block: make it gate 🔧

Until the competition: build, tune, log

  • 🔌 Pusher: cable upright in grey, from 3 different approach angles
  • 🎤 Carry claw: mic upright in target, release and retreat
  • 🔬 Ten grabs by hand: home(), grab(), write the angle down, release(). Write your spread, not your angle: under 10° build a run on it, 10–25° your claw rocks in its mounting, over 25° fix the build, not the code
  • 📋 Log every rep. Today’s log is tonight’s Accuracy score

Stage all four ways homing goes wrong. Every one of them passes on your desk, which is why you break it on purpose. 🎭

Break it What you see
home the wrong direction zero lands at open, and nothing closes again
duty_limit too high you hear the teeth skip, a stripped gear 🔊
duty_limit too low zero lands somewhere new, so homing is a lie
skip the wait(200) zero drifts, always the same way
  • Start every attempt with claw.home(). Position truth first, no exceptions
  • Miss? Say which trick failed: funnel, over-travel, or ratio. Then fix that

The longest block of the day — protect it, and note it now carries three jobs, not one. Teams work on their competition robots; the parts donor pool is open under the usual rules. Coach circulates with one diagnostic question only: “which trick failed?” — forcing the funnel/over-travel/ratio vocabulary to do diagnostic work on every miss. The rep log is not busywork: the daily head-to-head scores Accuracy from logged practice runs, and the week’s exit gate (both cables upright ≥8/10, mic in-target upright) is just this log grown up. If the coupling question from Act A bites here — a squad finds the mic claw disturbing the cable claw mid-sequence — that’s the day’s best possible problem: solve it on the real mission, with the coach watching the design conversation happen.

The homing table is a drill, not a demo — protect ten minutes for it. Squads split the four and stage them for each other. Supervise the duty_limit rows. Let them hear a real skip at duty_limit=100 on a re-geared claw once, briefly, then stop it; the sound is worth more than any warning, and it’s the same lesson the heavy-lift break-it opened the day with — torque has consequences at both ends of the chain. Do not let anyone sit at 100 for more than a second, and don’t run this on a build you can’t afford to re-teeth. The low side is subtler and more valuable: at 15 the jaws stop short of actually meeting, reset_angle(0) fires anyway, and now zero is almost right — worse than obviously wrong, because it passes on the bench and fails on the mat. Tie it back: a tuning knob found by feel, exactly like KP. The wrong-direction row is the most common: they flip a sign, the claw homes to open, and every grab() afterwards asks the motor to drive somewhere it already is — the fix is to flip Direction in their gripper file, not the numbers. Collect the four “what you’d see” answers on the board; that list is a diagnostic sheet for the rest of the season.

On the ten grabs: one reading always looks fine, so a single number cannot tell you whether a claw is competition-grade. Spread can. Make the board column say spread, not angle — a wide spread is a build defect and points at a specific fix (the claw rocks, the mount flexes, the object rolls before the jaws arrive) rather than at a number to fiddle with, so hold the line on the bottom row when someone wants to tune their way out of a 30° spread. Have them home, grab, write the angle down, release, and repeat — ten rows in a notebook, which is the whole experiment. Budget two minutes per squad, and have them run it twice: now, and again at the end of the day on a tired battery, which is when a claw that squeezes by force pulls away from one that merely stalled. If the two look close on a fresh battery, say so plainly rather than overselling; a coach who oversells a result the students can see is fine loses the argument they actually need to win.
